# FVReconstructedPressureGradient

## Description

FVReconstructedPressureGradient implements the flux-reconstruction idea proposed by
[!cite](aguerre2018oscillation) for segregated,
collocated finite-volume flow solvers. The method uses the corrected conservative face
fluxes to construct a cell velocity that does not contain the high-frequency modes
filtered out by face interpolation. It then recovers the cell pressure gradient required
to make the algebraic momentum equation reproduce that reconstructed velocity.

The method is intended specifically for momentum-pressure coupling. It does not define
the general physical pressure gradient used by diffusion corrections, boundary
corrections, diagnostics, or unrelated equations. Those operations should continue to
use an ordinary gradient method such as [FVGreenGaussGradient.md].

## Motivation

In a collocated finite-volume discretization, pressure and velocity are stored at cell
centers. Rhie–Chow interpolation suppresses pressure checkerboarding by constructing a
pressure-sensitive face flux. After the pressure correction, the resulting face flux is
locally conservative and generally smoother than the cell-centered velocity field.

The standard SIMPLE cell-velocity correction does not necessarily inherit that
smoothness. High-frequency components of a cell field can vanish when the field is
interpolated to faces. The pressure equation therefore cannot see all oscillatory
components that may remain in the cell-centered momentum quantities. Aguerre et al.
address this mismatch by treating the corrected face fluxes as the authoritative
velocity information and reconstructing a compatible cell velocity from them.

Let the volumetric velocity flux through face \(f\) of cell \(P\) be

\begin{equation}
F_f = \mathbf{u}_f \cdot \mathbf{S}_f ,
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbf{S}_f\) is the outward face-area vector. When RhieChowMassFlux stores a
mass flux, the corresponding velocity flux is obtained by dividing by the face density.

The reconstruction seeks a cell-centered velocity
\(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_P\) whose face-normal projections reproduce the corrected
fluxes as closely as possible.

## Face-to-cell reconstruction

### Weighted flux minimization

A zero-order reconstruction would approximate the flux generated by a cell velocity as

\begin{equation}
F_f^*(\mathbf{u}_P) = \mathbf{u}_P \cdot \mathbf{S}_f .
\end{equation}

Although this reconstructs a cell value from face data, Aguerre et al. show that the
zero-order form does not provide the accuracy required to preserve the formal order of
the flow solver. Their reconstruction includes a first-order Taylor contribution based
on the velocity gradient from the preceding corrector iteration:

\begin{equation}
F_f^*(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_P)
=
\left[
  \widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_P
  + \left(\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\,i}_P\right)_f
    \cdot\mathbf{d}_{Pf}
\right]\cdot\mathbf{S}_f ,
\end{equation}

where

- \(i\) denotes the previously stored corrector iterate;
- \(\mathbf{d}_{Pf}=\mathbf{x}_f-\mathbf{x}_P\) connects the cell and face centroids;
- \(\left(\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\,i}_P\right)_f\) is obtained by interpolating the
  neighboring cell velocity gradients to the face.

The reconstructed velocity minimizes the inverse-area-weighted flux difference

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}_P(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_P)
=
\sum_{f\in P}
\frac{1}{\lVert\mathbf{S}_f\rVert}
\left[
  F_f-F_f^*(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_P)
\right]^2 .
\end{equation}

Setting the derivative of this expression to zero gives the local system

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}_P\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_P=\mathbf{b}_P ,
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}_P
=
\sum_{f\in P}
\frac{\mathbf{S}_f\otimes\mathbf{S}_f}
     {\lVert\mathbf{S}_f\rVert}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{b}_P
=
\sum_{f\in P}
\left[
  F_f
  -
  \left(
    \left(\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\,i}_P\right)_f
    \cdot\mathbf{d}_{Pf}
  \right)\cdot\mathbf{S}_f
\right]
\frac{\mathbf{S}_f}{\lVert\mathbf{S}_f\rVert}.
\end{equation}

This is the Aguerre reconstruction used by the linear finite-volume segregated solver.
The matrix \(\mathbf{M}_P\) depends only on the face geometry, while the right-hand side
contains the current corrected fluxes and the lagged Taylor correction.

The use of the preceding velocity gradient is important. Directly inverting the same
interpolation used to construct the face values would recover the original oscillatory
cell field. The lagged gradient changes the inverse operation and introduces a
high-frequency damping effect analogous to the pressure damping introduced by
Rhie–Chow interpolation.

## Recovery of the momentum-coupling pressure gradient

Aguerre et al. reconstruct a cell velocity first and then alter the pressure gradient in
the algebraic momentum correction so that the corrected velocity equals the reconstructed
one. The pressure field itself is not reconstructed.

In the sign convention used by RhieChowMassFlux, the cell-velocity correction is

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}_P
=
-\left(\frac{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{A}}\right)_P
-\mathbf{A}^{-1}_P\nabla p_P ,
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}_P\) contains the inverse momentum diagonal for each component.
Requiring this relation to produce
\(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_P\) gives the reconstructed coupling gradient

\begin{equation}
\left(\nabla p\right)^{\mathrm{rec}}_{P,k}
=
\frac{
  -\widetilde{u}_{P,k}
  -\left(H/A\right)_{P,k}
}{
  \left(A^{-1}\right)_{P,k}
},
\end{equation}

for momentum component \(k\). If the inverse diagonal is zero, the configured base
gradient is used for that component.

The reconstructed gradient must be used consistently in two places:

1. the pressure source in each component of the momentum predictor; and
2. the removal of the pressure contribution when RhieChowMassFlux constructs
   \(\mathbf{H}/\mathbf{A}\).

Using different gradients in these operations would leave part of the pressure source
inside \(\mathbf{H}/\mathbf{A}\) and would invalidate the velocity–gradient relation
above.

## Relaxation and initialization

Before a reconstructed value is available, the coupling field is computed with
[!param](/FVGradientMethods/FVReconstructedPressureGradient/base_gradient_method).
The base method therefore supplies the first momentum predictor and the initial value of
the stored reconstructed field.

After a pressure correction has produced new conservative face fluxes, the reconstructed
candidate is blended into the stored coupling gradient:

\begin{equation}
\left(\nabla p\right)^{i+1}_{\mathrm{c}}
=
(1-\alpha)
\left(\nabla p\right)^i_{\mathrm{c}}
+
\alpha
\left(\nabla p\right)^{\mathrm{rec}},
\end{equation}

where
\(\alpha\) is
[!param](/FVGradientMethods/FVReconstructedPressureGradient/gradient_relaxation).
The stored field is updated once per pressure corrector. Repeated requests for the
gradient within the same SIMPLE iteration return the same values and do not apply the
relaxation again.

The cell velocity is subsequently updated from the same stored coupling gradient:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}_P
=
-\left(\frac{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{A}}\right)_P
-\mathbf{A}^{-1}_P
\left(\nabla p\right)_{\mathrm{c},P}.
\end{equation}

## SIMPLE update sequence

For a reconstructed-gradient solve, the relevant steps are:

1. Compute the base pressure gradient and initialize the coupling gradient.
2. Assemble and solve the momentum equations using the coupling gradient.
3. Construct \(\mathbf{H}/\mathbf{A}\) by removing that same gradient from the
   momentum right-hand side.
4. Solve the pressure-correction equation.
5. Update the pressure and conservative face mass fluxes.
6. Reconstruct \(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_P\) from the corrected face fluxes.
7. Recover and relax \(\left(\nabla p\right)^{\mathrm{rec}}\).
8. Correct the cell velocity with the relaxed coupling gradient.

The corrected face flux is the primary conservative quantity. The reconstructed cell
velocity and coupling gradient are derived from it.

## Accuracy and applicability

Aguerre et al. derive two accuracy requirements for the reconstruction operator. In
their tests, the Taylor-corrected reconstruction causes the round-trip
cell-to-face-to-cell velocity error to decay faster than the lower-order reconstruction
formulas, while reconstruction of a cell gradient from face-normal gradient fluxes
retains second-order behavior. These properties allow the added damping term to vanish
under refinement without reducing the second-order accuracy of the underlying solver.

These results depend on the mesh, gradient approximation, temporal discretization, and
consistent decomposition of the face flux into momentum and pressure contributions.
They should not be interpreted as a general guarantee for arbitrary distorted meshes.
In particular:

- \(\mathbf{M}_P\) must be nonsingular and sufficiently well conditioned;
- the lagged cell velocity gradients must be accurate enough for the Taylor correction;
- density used to convert mass flux to velocity flux must be consistent with the face
  flux construction;
- boundary faces require a compatible flux and gradient treatment;
- strongly nonorthogonal or skewed grids can reduce the accuracy of the reconstructed
  coupling gradient.

Ordinary Green–Gauss gradients should continue to be used for
nonorthogonal diffusion corrections and other spatial operators. The reconstructed
gradient should remain optional unless its behavior has been verified for the relevant
mesh and boundary treatment.
